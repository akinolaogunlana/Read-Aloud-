Every Browser's Built-In Read Aloud, Compared Honestly

Edge has the strongest native reader of the four. Safari doesn't really have one — it borrows Apple's system-wide accessibility tools. Chrome can't read a PDF at all without help. Firefox has nothing built in whatsoever. Here's what's actually true about each one.

Microsoft Edge — the strongest native option

Edge's Read Aloud is genuinely the best built-in text-to-speech feature of any major browser, and it's not close. Right-click any page (or select specific text first) and choose "Read aloud," or use the shortcut Ctrl+Shift+U. It reads full webpages, selected text, and — unusually for a browser — actual PDFs opened in Edge, without needing an extension.

Edge ships with Microsoft's neural "Natural" voices built in (Aria, Jenny, Guy, and others), which sound noticeably more human than older robotic TTS voices, across roughly 50 languages. A separate mode called Immersive Reader (triggered with F9) adds line-focus highlighting, syllable splitting, and a picture dictionary on top of Read Aloud, though it only works on article-style pages rather than everywhere Read Aloud itself works. It's also available in Edge's mobile app on iOS and Android.

Safari — there's no dedicated button, because it's not really a Safari feature

This trips people up: Safari doesn't have its own read-aloud feature. What it has is access to macOS and iOS's system-wide accessibility tools, which happen to work inside Safari the same way they work inside Mail, Notes, or any other app.

On a Mac, turn on Speak Selection under System Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content, then select any text and press Option+Escape (or right-click → Speech → Start Speaking). On iPhone or iPad, the same toggle lives under Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content, and you get two options: Speak Selection (highlight text, tap Speak) or Speak Screen (a two-finger swipe down from the top of the screen reads everything currently visible). Voice quality depends entirely on which system voice you've installed — the defaults are functional but flat; Apple's premium neural voices sound considerably better and are a separate download.

Chrome — Reading Mode helps, but it has real gaps

Chrome has no single "read the whole page" button the way Edge does. What it has is Reading Mode, accessible from the side panel (Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+S), which strips a page down to clean article text and reads it aloud with sentence-by-sentence highlighting — but only on pages Chrome recognizes as articles in the first place.

The bigger gap: Chrome cannot read a PDF aloud natively at all. Open a PDF in Chrome and there's no listen option anywhere — PDFs are treated as files, not articles, so Reading Mode never triggers on them. Chrome also uses whichever voices happen to be installed at the operating-system level, same as Safari, rather than shipping its own like Edge does. Most people relying on Chrome for serious listening end up installing a third-party extension.

Firefox — no native read-aloud at all

Firefox is the outlier: there's no built-in text-to-speech feature of any kind, native or accessibility-adjacent, comparable to what the other three browsers offer. Anyone wanting to listen to a page in Firefox is relying entirely on a third-party extension from Mozilla's add-on store.

What none of these do — even Edge

All four are built to read a page or a selection out loud, and that's genuinely useful. None of them are built for the specific job of proofreading your own writing before you publish it. Concretely, none of the four:

  • Let you flag a specific line while listening and export the full list afterward as a checklist
  • Calculate a readability score (Flesch-Kincaid or otherwise) for what you're about to publish
  • Automatically detect repeated words, double spaces, or spacing errors before you even start listening
  • Work identically whether you're on Chrome, Edge, Safari, or Firefox — each browser's method is different, and switching browsers means relearning the shortcut
  • Handle a .docx or .epub manuscript directly with real paragraph-level structure the way a dedicated tool built around document proofing does

If you just want to listen to an article on your lunch break, Edge's Read Aloud is genuinely excellent and there's no reason to look further. If you're proofing your own manuscript, essay, or report before it goes out into the world, that's a different job — one none of these four were built for.
